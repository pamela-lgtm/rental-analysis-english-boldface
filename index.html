What has happened to this market

Two things changed at once. Sales stalled, so builders cut prices — the new-construction townhomes in this comp set sold 5% to 12% below their original asking prices. And owners who could not sell converted to rentals rather than keep paying a mortgage on an empty house.

Both streams landed in the same rental pool. Fourteen townhomes leased in the fourteen weeks to September 9 — about four a month — while fourteen more sit unleased today. That is roughly three and a half months of standing inventory ahead of any new listing, unless its price moves it to the front of the line.

The competition is still growing, which is why timing matters. A price that looks competitive today looks ordinary in six weeks. Anyone who lists high planning to reduce later ends up reducing into a bigger field than they started with — and family relocations slow sharply after August, so the pool of renters is shrinking at the same time.

How we read the comparables

Size barely moves rent here

Every three-bedroom lease except the outlier landed between $2,450 and $2,900, across homes from 1,447 to 1,755 square feet. Renters in this corridor are buying a three-bedroom Lake Nona townhome at a monthly number, not paying by the foot. At $2,650 your home rents at $1.54 per square foot — exactly what 11902 Architecture Alley achieved, and the median for the larger half of this set. Your size is an advantage in getting chosen, not a reason to ask for more.

The fourth bedroom is worth about $100 — and no speed

Comparing identical square footages, four-bedroom homes earned $67 to $175 more per month, so we discount the five four-bedroom comparables by $100 to read them as three-bedroom equivalents. That premium buys nothing in time: the four-bedroom that earned the biggest difference needed 110 days, and all three four-bedroom homes on the market today are still sitting at 33, 37 and 57 days.

Gated status is not a factor; amenities set the ceiling, not the price

Not one of the 30 townhomes is in a gated community. There is no gated product competing here, so there is no premium available for a gate and no penalty for its absence.

Storey Park does have the deepest amenity package of the three communities — two clubhouses, resort and lap pools, splash pad, spa, fitness center, tennis, dog park, trails and a restaurant. That advantage is real, and it is why we are recommending above the Starwood evidence rather than at it. But the five Storey Park homes asking $2,695 to $2,850 have the same clubhouses and the same pools, and four are sitting. Amenities earn your home the right to be considered at the top of the corridor. They do not get it leased.

How we lease it: unfurnished, 12 months, pets negotiable

Unfurnished

All 30 listings in this analysis are unfurnished. There is no furnished townhome competing in this corridor, so no local evidence a furnished premium exists. Furnishing would also pull your home out of the long-term renter pool — the families relocating for Medical City, the airport and the schools — and drop it into a much thinner corporate and short-stay pool. With fourteen unleased competitors, shrinking the audience is the last thing we want to do. It also puts every item in the home onto the lease, with inventory lists and wear disputes at move-out, for a premium the market has not demonstrated.

Pets must be negotiable

We want to be direct with you about this, because it is a condition of our management rather than a suggestion: we do not take on a property with a blanket no-pet policy. Pets need to stay negotiable, case by case, with us screening every applicant and every animal.

The reason is the size of your applicant pool. Roughly two-thirds of American renter households have a pet. A closed door does not filter applicants — it deletes most of them before they ever inquire. With fourteen unleased competitors and five listings stacked inside your own community, that is a cost you cannot absorb. Look at where the closed listings sit today: 10011 Gazebo at 42 days, 11763 Boldface at 37, 11549 Straits at 56, 11546 Buoy Point at 57.

There is also a legal reality worth knowing. Assistance animals are not pets under fair housing law, and a qualified request must be accommodated regardless of policy. A no-pet rule does not keep animals out of the home. It only keeps out the applicants who would have paid you for the privilege — and they do pay: comparables here charge a $250 to $500 non-refundable pet fee, a $200 to $400 deposit, and $20 to $50 a month per animal.

Negotiable does not mean unrestricted. You keep the controls: a limit on the number of pets, a weight cap, breed review, a third-party pet profile required from every applicant including those with no pet, a non-refundable fee and monthly pet rent. That gives us the full applicant pool, additional income, and a documented record of every animal in your home.

Why we are pricing under the evidence

A listing price is a marketing decision, not a valuation. What your home is worth and what we should list it at are two different questions, and only the second one gets it leased.

The evidence supports $2,700 to $2,800 for a Storey Park three-bedroom on paper. We are recommending $2,650, deliberately underneath it, so you are the obvious value in your own community. 9722 Character Drive is asking $2,695 for 1,518 square feet built the same year — you would be offering 200 more square feet for $45 less, at the lowest price on the Storey Park board. That is the listing a renter screenshots and sends to their spouse.

The vacancy arithmetic is why we will not chase the higher number. At $2,650 every vacant day costs $87.

Chasing an extra $100 a month takes over two years to recover if it costs one additional vacant month — and a 12-month lease does not give you two years. On a single term, that trade is a loss before it starts.

We are not going to estimate how long it will take to lease. Nobody can know that, and an invented number would only mislead you. Instead we measure: inquiries, showings booked and showings actually attended over the first five days. If that activity is not strong enough for us to choose a well-qualified renter rather than accept whoever appears, we move to $2,550 — not a token cut, a number renters notice. If the market says that is not enough, we go to $2,495 and keep reading it. $2,495 is a step, not a bottom; only the market knows where the bottom is, and our commitment is to keep reacting to it rather than defending a number we picked in advance.

The purpose of generating a pool is selection, not acceptance. One application means you take that applicant or stay empty. Five applications mean we screen, verify income and rental history, and place the household most likely to pay on time, stay the term, and hand the home back in good condition. That is where the money in a rental is actually made.
